ARVIEWPORT — HOW IT WORKS

WHAT YOU'RE LOOKING AT

The screen pretends to be a pane of glass into an alternate reality — an AR viewport. It is a full 360° world: turn on the spot and the horizon slides past, tilt down to the ground, tilt up to the sky. The selectors combine freely: four places (a city, open prairie, a desert, a harbor), three times of day (each place is repainted with its own day / dusk / night palette, lights coming on after dark, stars and moon at night, sun and blue sky by day), six kinds of weather, and a roster of beings roaming the far side of the glass. In rain, every visible drop is an individual particle: drops land, bead up, merge, and — once heavy enough for the current tilt of your actual phone — run downhill in stuttering rivulets. And whatever the weather leaves on the glass, you can wipe off with a pressed finger.

WEATHER & THE WIPE

Weather works on both sides of the glass. On the world side: rain falls, snow drifts down as world-anchored flake fields, dust storms and fog swallow the horizon at different rates, and wildfire puts a flickering orange glow, rising embers and a smoke pall over one quarter of the horizon. On the glass side, each weather deposits its own dirt into a grime map: brown dust film, creeping frost from the edges and bottom, gray ash flecks — and rain gradually washes old grime away. The pane also lives its own life over time: it starts freshly wiped, then steam re-condenses continuously (fastest in fog and rain, at night on cold glass; slowly even on a clear day) and everyday dust keeps settling, so the view gradually mists and dirties until you wipe it again — the page will nudge you if you've let it fog over too long. A pressed finger wipes: it clears grime, clears the condensation fog, and sweeps droplets aside along the stroke. On a phone with motion sensors any touch wipes (the view is head-driven); on desktop the 🧹 button switches the drag between looking and wiping.

BEINGS

The beings selector (default none) populates the world with silhouettes that live in a separate overlay canvas mapped exactly like the panorama band, redrawn every other frame: walking people with swinging limbs, grazing deer that raise their heads, a flapping bird flock, a slow tall monster that sometimes stops and turns its glowing eyes on you, or a dragon circling the sky that occasionally breathes fire. Because the overlay shares the band mapping, beings hold their compass positions as you turn, and they blur behind fogged glass just like the world does.

THE RENDERING STACK (TWO LAYERS)

  • CPU layer: a JavaScript particle simulation draws each drop as a soft radial-gradient blob into a half-resolution offscreen 2D canvas. That canvas is a height field — brightness means water thickness, not colour. Runner drops are drawn as three overlapping blobs (head + two trailing) to make teardrop shapes.
  • GPU layer: each frame the height field is uploaded as a texture to a WebGL2 fragment shader. For every pixel the shader builds a view ray from the screen position and the phone's orientation matrix, looks that ray up in the world (below), then uses the height field's gradient as a surface normal to refract: inside a drop the ray is bent against the normal and samples the sharp world (drops act as little lenses, so they look crisper than the fogged glass around them), plus a specular glint from a fixed key light, a Fresnel-ish rim, and an inner shadow on the far side.

The world is a 360° night panorama painted once at load into a 2048×896 canvas band covering elevations −29°…+60° (skyline silhouettes, lit windows, neon, street-lamp and light-pollution glows, painted stars), box-blurred at two extra strengths for the fogged and wiped glass looks. Outside the band the shader takes over procedurally: an octahedral-mapped hash star field with twinkle plus an analytic moon disc and halo above, and noise-mottled near-black ground below — so looking straight up or down never runs out of texture. Fogged glass shows the heavy blur with a milky lift; wiped tracks show the lighter blur; drop lenses sample sharp. No image assets — every pixel is generated in the page.

THE DROPLET SIMULATION

  • Condensation: ~1,500 static micro-droplets are pre-rendered once; a persistent "wet map" canvas erases them where runners have passed, and slowly fades so mist re-condenses over a minute or so.
  • Stick-slip: every beaded drop has a random stiction threshold. It breaks loose when |g_tangential| × r² beats that threshold — so at a slight ~20° tilt only the heaviest drops creep, at steeper tilts everything runs. Runners also make random micro-pauses, which is what real drops do (contact-line pinning).
  • Rivulets: a runner sheds mass as it goes, depositing a trail of tiny beads and losing radius until it re-pins. Runners are steered toward beads slightly ahead of them (a cheap spatial-hash cone search), so later drops follow earlier tracks — that's why channels form.
  • Merging: overlapping drops combine area (r = √(r²+r’²)) and the merged drop gets a speed kick, like real coalescence cascades.
  • Face-up splatter: when the phone lies flat facing the sky, tangential gravity vanishes: nothing can run. Instead the impact rate rises and each hit spawns a crown — an expanding ring drawn briefly into the height field — plus a scatter of ejecta micro-drops around a new bead. Face the screen at the ground and the rain can't reach the glass at all, so the drops slowly drip away.

ORIENTATION: ONE MATRIX FOR VIEW AND GRAVITY

The deviceorientation angles build one rotation matrix, R = RZ(α)·RX(β)·RY(γ)·RZ(screen angle), low-pass smoothed and re-orthonormalised each frame. That single matrix does two jobs: it rotates each pixel's view ray into the world (so the panorama, stars and ground track your real heading and tilt), and its third row is the gravity vector in screen coordinates (so the drops run, bead or splatter consistently with what you see — look down through a flat pane and the same math that shows you the ground also makes the rain splatter instead of trickle). iOS requires a permission prompt for motion data — that's what the first tap is for. With no sensor (desktop), dragging turns and tilts a virtual phone instead.

APPROXIMATIONS & LIMITATIONS

  • The refraction is screen-space fakery: a normal-driven UV offset into a flat background photo of the scene. Real drops invert and compress the whole scene behind them; here the lens look is approximated by a large offset against the normal, so you get the feel of it without the true inverted image.
  • The water isn't fluid-simulated — no surface tension solver, no Navier–Stokes. Blob sprites + stick-slip rules are a phenomenological imitation of drop behaviour. Drop shapes are always round-ish; real crawling drops develop asymmetric contact lines.
  • Height is a painter's trick (gradient blobs), so two touching drops blend heights instead of forming a real meniscus bridge before merging.
  • No caustics: real drops focus light into bright crescents on whatever is behind the glass.
  • The height-field texture is half resolution for performance, so very fine trails soften.
  • The world is an infinitely-distant panorama: no parallax — walking around your hill changes nothing, only rotation does. The skyline band is one texture, so very tall thin details soften; the azimuth seam is hidden in the darkest stretch of countryside.
  • On iOS, alpha is relative to where the phone pointed at page load, not true north — the skyline's compass position is arbitrary (and can drift), but turning always pans it believably. Near straight up/down the euler angles gimbal-flip; matrix smoothing hides most of the wobble.
  • Weather is stylised: snow and embers are hash-grid particle fields in azimuth/elevation space (they fall in world space but have no depth layers), fog and dust are elevation-graded colour mixes, and the wildfire never spreads. Beings are hand-painted 2D silhouettes on the panorama band — they never come closer or change apparent distance, and they walk through scenery. The scene repaint on a selector change is a full texture rebuild, so expect a one-frame hitch.

TOOLING

Hand-written single-file HTML/JS/GLSL — no libraries, no build step, no image or audio assets. Sound (tap 🔊) is WebAudio: looping filtered brown-noise for the rain bed plus randomly scheduled band-passed noise grains for patter, both shaped live by tilt mode.
